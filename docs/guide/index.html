How LIMAZ decides, records and defends every expense

A company's backend submits an expense. A policy engine approves it, flags it, or rejects it. A manager resolves what gets flagged. A card provider can submit one too, over a signed webhook. This guide follows each of those paths from the first byte to the stored row — with the real screens, the real code, and the alternatives that were rejected on the way.

Part 00

Orientation

Four kinds of caller reach this system, and each one proves who it is in a different way. Everything else in this guide follows from that.

The one rule everything else protects

The invariant

A company can only ever see its own data, and the company is resolved from the verified credential, never from the request. Two routes take a company id in the path; both are platform-administrator routes where the id is a selector applied after the role check, not a claim of identity.

Two words that are not the same word

The single most important idea in the data model is that decision and status are separate columns, and neither overwrites the other.

ColumnWho writes itValuesChanges later?
decision The policy engine, once, at submission APPROVE REVIEW REJECT Never. It is history
status The engine, then possibly a manager APPROVED PENDING_REVIEW REJECTED Yes, once, when a review is resolved

So an expense can read “Policy: Review” and “Approved” at the same time, and that is not a contradiction — it is the audit trail saying the machine flagged it and a human then let it through. You will see exactly that screen in Part 01.

Part 01

The product, screen by screen

Every screenshot below was taken from the running system on 21 November 2026, against Delta Consulting — one of the two seeded companies. Its policy auto-approves up to SAR 2,000.00, flags more than 5 expenses in 30 minutes from one employee, and rejects ALCOHOL and GAMBLING outright. Nile Logistics, the other seeded company, has deliberately different numbers, which is how the tests prove the policy is per company rather than global.

Story 1 — Getting in

There is one way in. No route in the company workspace renders anything for an unauthenticated caller: asking for /dashboard without a session lands on the sign-in page with a reason attached, and the reason is why the page explains itself instead of just appearing.

Why one message

“No such user” and “wrong password” are different answers to an attacker: the first one confirms an address is registered. The login schema's minimum password length is deliberately loose for the same reason — a “too short” validation error would be a third answer that leaks the same thing.

Story 2 — What the manager sees first

The totals reconcile: 5 + 1 + 3 = 9. They are counted by current status, which is why an expense the policy flagged and a manager then approved counts as approved here while still showing “Review” in its decision column.

Story 3 — Why an expense got the answer it got

This is the screen that carries the product's argument. An expense detail does not say “rejected”; it says which rule fired, what the engine measured at the time, and what the policy said at that moment.

The part that matters

“Policy in force when this was judged” is a snapshot stored on the expense row, not a lookup of today's policy. Change the limit tomorrow and this page still shows SAR 2,000.00, because that is what the expense was actually measured against. A reviewer can change the policy and watch old decisions stay exactly as they were.

Story 4 — A human decides

The dialog traps focus, closes on Escape, and returns focus to the button that opened it. That was verified in a real browser rather than assumed from the component library.

Story 5 — The rules themselves

Stated on the page itself

Changes apply to new evaluations. Expenses already submitted keep the policy and the decision they were given. The UI says this under the save button rather than leaving a manager to discover it.

Story 6 — What a manager cannot do

Why 404 and not 403

403 confirms the row exists. Walk a list of ids with a 403/404 oracle and you learn exactly how many expenses your competitor has. The same answer for “no such expense” and “not yours” gives an attacker nothing, and the wording in the UI matches: “it does not exist, or it belongs to another company.”

The gate runs in both directions. A platform administrator asking for a company workspace is refused too, because an administrator belongs to no company and a workspace without a company is not a thing this system can render.

Story 7 — Onboarding a company, end to end

A platform administrator can take a company from nothing to working without touching the database. That was not true before the final feature: creating a manager was the one step that had no screen, and the company detail page used to carry a warning saying so.

Note what is not on that page. The API key shows a truncated prefix and nothing more: keys are stored as a SHA-256 hash, so the full value genuinely cannot be retrieved. The webhook panel shows a delivery id and a secret version, never the signing secret.

Try to set them anyway

Sending "role": "PLATFORM_ADMIN" or a different "companyId" in the request body is a 400 that creates nothing — not a field that is parsed and then ignored. The schema is .strict() and neither field is declared, so the request dies at the parse. “Ignored” is a property of today's service code; “refused” is a property of the schema and survives whatever the service does next.

Story 8 — A card provider submits, without any account

The optional bonus. A card provider has no API key and no session. It authenticates on a per-request HMAC signature, and the sandbox simulator lets you watch the whole path without a provider, a card, or any money.

Why the simulator is not a shortcut

It does not call the expense service directly. It builds a synthetic provider event, signs it server-side, and sends a real signed HTTP request to the real endpoint — which is why its failure presets actually fail.

Part 02

Inside a request

What actually happens between the first byte arriving and a row existing. Everything in this part is the real pipeline, in the real order, with the code that implements it.

The order of the gates, and why it is that order

apps/api/src/app.module.ts:87global guards, in order
// Nest executes APP_GUARD providers in registration order.
{ provide: APP_GUARD, useClass: CsrfGuard },
{ provide: APP_GUARD, useClass: AuthenticationGuard },
{ provide: APP_GUARD, useClass: AuthorizationGuard },
{ provide: APP_FILTER, useClass: HttpExceptionFilter },

CSRF runs first, before authentication resolves. That ordering is deliberate: the CSRF check is a property of the request, not of the caller, so a request that could never be honoured is refused without a database lookup and without a bcrypt comparison. It is also why a state-changing request with no credential and no x-limaz-client header answers 403 rather than 401.

A correction worth recording

An independent QA pass reported that the 403 applies to every route. It does not — CsrfGuard guards state-changing methods only, so a GET with no credential answers 401 with or without the header. A safe method is not what CSRF protects against.

How the company is resolved — one helper, every query

apps/api/src/common/tenant.ts:31the tenant predicate
export function tenantScope(principal: Principal): { companyId: string } {
  if (principal.companyId === null) {
    // A platform admin has no tenant of their own. Reaching here means a
    // company-scoped query ran on an admin's behalf without the admin naming
    // the company - which would silently have meant "no rows" or, worse, "all
    // rows". Admin endpoints take an explicit company id instead.
    throw new ForbiddenException({ error: 'tenant_required', ... });
  }

  return { companyId: principal.companyId };
}

It throws rather than returning an empty filter, and that is the whole point. An empty filter means either “no rows” or “all rows” depending on how it is spread into a where clause, and the second one is a cross-tenant leak that looks like working code. Because there is exactly one helper, grep tenantScope finds every company-scoped query in the codebase.

The honest limit

Nothing prevents a future query from omitting it. PostgreSQL row-level security would; it was rejected for lack of Prisma support, the per-transaction SET LOCAL requirement, and friction under transaction-mode pooling. Recorded as risk R-20, with a negative-control test that fails when the predicate is removed.

Part 03

The five cycles

Five paths carry everything this system does. Each one is traced here from the caller to the committed row, with the code that makes the guarantee hold.

Cycle 1 — Submission: from HTTP to a decided expense

The three rules, and which one wins

Cycle 2 — The same expense, submitted twice

Networks retry. A company backend that times out and sends the same expense again must not create two. The guarantee is a database constraint, not application logic.

apps/api/prisma/schema.prisma:298the idempotency key
@@unique([companyId, source, externalId])

Three columns, and each earns its place. companyId means two companies may use the same reference without colliding. externalId is the client's own identifier. source is the namespace — API or CARD_WEBHOOK — so a card transaction id can never collide with a reference a backend chose.

The third case

Same externalId, different body is neither a retry nor a new expense. It is a 409, and the original expense is left exactly as it was. Silently accepting it would let a client overwrite a decided expense by reusing a reference.

Cycle 3 — Two managers click Approve at the same moment

The resolve path is one conditional UPDATE. There is no read first, so there is no window between reading and writing for a second manager to slip into.

apps/api/src/reviews/review-resolution.service.ts:107the atomic claim
UPDATE "reviews"
   SET state = 'RESOLVED',
       outcome = ${outcome}::"ReviewOutcome",
       "resolvedByUserId" = ${userId}::uuid,
       "resolvedAt" = resolution.at,
       "updatedAt" = resolution.at
  FROM (SELECT clock_timestamp() AS at) AS resolution
 WHERE "reviews".id = ${reviewId}::uuid
   AND "reviews"."companyId" = ${companyId}::uuid
   AND "reviews".state = 'OPEN'      <-- the whole race lives here
RETURNING "reviews"."expenseId"

AND state = 'OPEN' is the entire concurrency control. PostgreSQL serialises the two updates on the row; the first sets RESOLVED, and the second then matches zero rows. The winner gets 200; the loser gets a 409 that names the decision that already happened, so the manager learns what their colleague chose rather than just being told no.

Two details worth defending

clock_timestamp() is computed once in the FROM clause and written to both columns, so resolvedAt and updatedAt cannot disagree — two separate calls would return two different instants. And updatedAt is set explicitly because Prisma's @updatedAt is applied by the client, which a raw statement bypasses; without it the column would silently keep its creation value.

Zero rows updated is not automatically “already resolved”. It is one of four things — no such review, another company's review, already resolved, or a genuine conflict — and they get four different answers. Treating every zero-row update as a successful retry would report success for a review that never existed.

The expense update rides in the same transaction, which answers the obvious follow-up: what if the review updates and the expense does not? Nothing is observable to any other transaction until COMMIT, so the state where a review is resolved while its expense is still pending never exists. It only exists if the two writes are split across transactions.

Cycle 4 — A card provider delivers a settled transaction

Why the signature covers the raw bytes

apps/api/src/webhooks/webhook-crypto.ts:179the signed material
export function signedPayload(timestamp: string, rawBody: Buffer): Buffer {
  return Buffer.concat([Buffer.from(`${timestamp}.`, 'utf8'), rawBody]);
}

export function computeSignature(secret, timestamp, rawBody): string {
  return createHmac('sha256', secret)
    .update(signedPayload(timestamp, rawBody))
    .digest('hex');
}
The mistake this avoids

JSON.parse followed by JSON.stringify is not the identity function. It normalises whitespace, drops insignificant formatting, re-encodes unicode escapes and renders 1.0 as 1. A verifier built on the parsed object fails every pretty-printed delivery — and, far worse, can be made to verify a body that differs from the one the application then acts on. The app is booted with rawBody: true so the exact received bytes survive.

The separator earns its place too. Without the dot, timestamp 1 with body 23{...} and timestamp 12 with body 3{...} would produce identical signed strings.

Two deduplication layers, and why one is not enough

LayerConstraintStops
Delivery @@unique([companyId, providerEventId]) The provider sending the same event again after a timeout
Effect @@unique([companyId, source, externalId]) Two different events describing one transaction becoming two expenses

A retry is the same event id. A re-settlement notice is a different event id for the same transaction id. Only the second layer catches the second case, and only the first layer lets the system answer a redelivery without doing the work again.

Claiming an event without two workers taking it

apps/api/src/webhooks/webhook-worker.service.ts:238claimOne()
UPDATE webhook_events
SET status = 'PROCESSING',
    "claimedAt" = now(),
    attempts = attempts + 1        -- incremented AT CLAIM TIME
WHERE id = (
  SELECT id FROM webhook_events
  WHERE (status = 'PENDING' AND "nextAttemptAt" <= now())
     OR (status = 'PROCESSING'
         AND "claimedAt" < now() - make_interval(secs => ${leaseSeconds}))
  ORDER BY "nextAttemptAt" ASC, "receivedAt" ASC
  FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED           -- a competitor steps over, never waits
  LIMIT 1
)
RETURNING ...

The row is locked by the SELECT and changed by the surrounding UPDATE in the same statement. A second worker's SKIP LOCKED steps over it instead of waiting, so both workers make progress and neither gets the same event.

attempts is incremented when the event is claimed, not when it completes. An event that crashes the worker every time still advances toward its bound; counting on completion would retry it forever. The second OR branch is crash recovery: a PROCESSING row whose lease has expired is reclaimed, with no heartbeat and no external supervisor.

What happens if the worker dies mid-expense

The expense may already be committed. The lease expires, another attempt claims the event, and calls the same submission service — which finds the externalId already stored and returns the existing expense. No new code was written for this. It is the idempotency guarantee from Cycle 2, and it is the real reason the worker calls the same service instead of its own.

Part 04

Decisions, and what they beat

Every choice below had at least one reasonable alternative. What follows is what was considered, what was picked, and the reason — including the cases where the rejected option is genuinely better on some axis and was turned down anyway.

1 — Two columns for the outcome, not one

One status column rejected

Simpler schema. But a manager approving a flagged expense overwrites the fact that it was ever flagged — the audit question “did the policy catch this?” becomes unanswerable.

A status history table rejected

Fully general, and the right answer for a workflow with many transitions. Here there are exactly two writers and one transition, so it buys generality nobody asked for and makes the common read a join.

decision + status chosen

The machine's verdict is immutable history; the human outcome is separate. Both are one column read, and the UI can show “Policy: Review” beside “Approved” without explaining a contradiction.

2 — Money as an integer of minor units

float / double rejected

0.1 + 0.2 is not 0.3. A comparison against a limit is exactly where that bites, and the error is silent.

NUMERIC(12,2) rejected

Correct arithmetic, and a defensible choice. It arrives in JavaScript as a string or a Decimal object, so every comparison needs a library and every serialisation needs a decision.

INTEGER minor units chosen

SAR 125.00 is 12500. Exact, comparable with >, and JSON-safe. The ceiling is the int4 limit — SAR 21,474,836.47 for one expense — which is a stated limit, not an accident.

3 — API keys hashed, webhook secrets encrypted

These look inconsistent and are not. The difference is what the server has to do with the value.

API keyWebhook signing secret
What the caller sendsthe key itselfa MAC computed with the secret
To verify, the server needsa one-way digest to comparethe key material itself
So it is stored asSHA-256 hashAES-256-GCM ciphertext
Database dump alone is enough?NoNo — the key lives outside the database
Stated plainly

Encryption is weaker than hashing, and this is recorded as risk R-35 rather than presented as equivalent. No digest can stand in for HMAC key material — verification means recomputing the MAC, which requires the secret. The mitigation is that an attacker needs both the database and WEBHOOK_ENCRYPTION_KEY, and the upgrade path is an external KMS where the key never enters the process.

The company id is used as the additional authenticated data, so a secret copied from one company's row into another's fails to decrypt rather than silently working — which is the injection somebody with database write access would actually try.

4 — Serialising the frequency rule

SERIALIZABLE isolation rejected

Correct, and it would also catch anomalies nobody anticipated. It pushes retry handling into every caller and penalises the whole transaction, including reads that never needed it.

An application mutex rejected

Works on one process. Two API containers each hold their own, and the guarantee silently disappears the day the deployment scales out.

pg_advisory_xact_lock chosen

Scoped to (company, employee) so unrelated traffic never contends, held by the database so every container shares it, and released by COMMIT, ROLLBACK or a crash.

apps/api/src/expenses/frequency-lock.ts:64two arguments, not one string
await tx.$executeRaw`SELECT pg_advisory_xact_lock(hashtext(${companyId}), hashtext(${employeeId}))`;

Two 32-bit arguments give a 264 key space and need no separator. hashtext(a || ':' || b) would give 32 bits — a birthday collision at roughly 77,000 distinct keys — and would need a separator that cannot occur in an employee id. A collision here can only make two unrelated pairs wait on each other; it cannot produce a wrong decision, because every query inside the lock still filters on the real ids. The lock decides who waits, never who sees what.

The transaction-scoped variant matters too. pg_advisory_lock is released only by an explicit unlock, so a crash would leak it onto a pooled connection and poison that connection for every later request — a latent outage under PgBouncer.

5 — The frequency window looks only backwards

Re-evaluate affected expenses rejected

Every insert could rewrite N decisions and open reviews retroactively. Decisions stop being stable, which breaks the audit trail the rest of the system is built to keep.

Symmetric window [t−60, t+60] rejected

Semantically wrong: it would count two expenses 118 minutes apart as being “within 60 minutes”.

Sliding sub-window search deferred

Correct. Needs a scan of a two-hour band and reasoning about every 60-minute sub-window containing t. Recorded as the upgrade path.

[spentAt − N, spentAt] chosen

Both bounds inclusive, measured on when the money was spent. Simple, stable, and explainable to the manager reading the rule hit.

The limitation this creates — risk R-11, open

An expense whose spentAt is earlier than one already decided does not see it, so it can be auto-approved although it is logically the fourth in its own window. This was described for a long time as a back-dating risk, which understated it: the real trigger is arrival order differing from spentAt order, and the commonest cause is ordinary concurrency. A burst of ten simultaneous submissions against a limit of three was measured auto-approving three to seven, varying by run.

The advisory lock does not help here and is not claimed to. Serialising writers does not change what each one is allowed to see, and a serialised transaction still evaluates only backwards.

6 — 404 rather than 403 across tenants

403 Forbidden rejected

Semantically honest — the row exists and you may not have it. That honesty is the problem: 403 versus 404 is an existence oracle, so walking a list of ids counts a competitor's expenses.

404 Not Found chosen

One answer for “no such row” and “not yours”. The id never reaches a query without the tenant predicate, so the server genuinely does not know which case it was.

The UI wording matches the guarantee rather than softening it: “It does not exist, or it belongs to another company.”

7 — Opaque session tokens, not JWTs

JWT rejected

Stateless and fashionable. It cannot be revoked before it expires, which is exactly what this system needs when an account is compromised or a manager leaves.

Opaque token, SHA-256 at rest chosen

A row that can be deleted. Absolute expiry is checked in SQL, so an expired session cannot be loaded at all. HttpOnly, Secure, SameSite=Lax, first-party.

8 — Same-origin proxy instead of CORS

There is deliberately no app.enableCors(). CORS is not a lock the server applies; it is permission the server grants. With no CORS headers the browser's default — deny — stands, which is the strictest posture available, and enabling CORS could only ever open access that is currently closed.

It is not needed because the browser never makes a cross-origin request: Next.js proxies /api/* server-side, so from the browser's point of view everything is same-origin. A test asserts that no access-control-allow-origin header is ever sent, because its presence would mean the deployment is no longer same-origin.

9 — The administrator types the first password

Server-generated password rejected

The machinery already exists for API keys. But the response then carries a plaintext credential, needing no-store, a one-time reveal and a copy affordance — and a lost response leaves an account nobody can sign in to.

Administrator chooses it chosen

Somebody carries the first password out of band either way, because this deployment sends no email. This way the response carries no credential at all — nothing to leak into a proxy log or an error reporter.

Two bounds, each for a reason. At least 12 characters, because this endpoint sets policy for a new credential — unlike login, whose bound is loose so “too short” cannot confirm an address exists. At most 72 bytes, because bcrypt hashes 72 and silently discards the rest: accepting a longer passphrase would create an account with far less entropy than the person believes they set. Bytes, not characters, because that is what bcrypt counts — a 40-character Arabic passphrase is already over the limit in UTF-8.

10 — A PostgreSQL inbox, not a message broker

Process synchronously rejected

The assessment asks for asynchronous processing, and for good reason: policy evaluation inside the provider's request means a slow evaluation becomes a delivery timeout and a retry storm.

Redis / SQS / RabbitMQ rejected

The right answer at volume. Here it adds a second durable store to operate, and a new failure mode: the event is committed to the queue but not to the database, or the reverse.

A table, claimed with SKIP LOCKED chosen

One store, so acceptance and durability are the same commit. Honest about what it is not: polling rather than push, no priorities, no dead-letter queue beyond status = FAILED.

What happens when the queue is down

There is no separate queue to be down. The inbox is a table in the same database that acceptance writes to, so if it is unavailable the 202 is never sent — and the provider, which retries until it gets a 2xx, keeps the event and sends it again. Nothing is lost by failing loudly at the front door. The dangerous design is the opposite one: answer 202, then try to enqueue.

11 — The gap that was explained rather than built

Risk R-38, open and deliberate

The assessment asks how you would find events the provider never delivered. This system cannot, on its own — an event that never arrived leaves no trace here, and nothing in the repository reconciles against the provider. Three approaches are ranked rather than implemented:

  1. Id-gap detection — only works if event ids are sequential, and evt_8f2c1 gives no reason to think they are.
  2. A reconciliation job against a provider listing API — the real answer. It needs an endpoint this integration does not have, and inventing one would be fabricating a contract.
  3. A liveness alert on companies that have gone quiet — cheap, weak, and genuinely useful.

Part 05

The hard questions

The things a reviewer actually asks, each with what the system does and the test that proves it. Every answer here was executed against the running stack, not inferred from the code.

Getting at another company's data

I put another company's expense id in the URL. What do I get?

404, never 403 — and nothing was read. The id goes into the WHERE clause beside companyId, so the query returns no rows rather than returning a row that is then checked. A post-fetch check has already pulled another tenant's data into the process and is one refactor away from leaking it.

The same holds for review ids, key ids and company ids — including through the admin path selector. Revoking another company's key through your company's URL is a 404, and the key still works afterwards.

What if I put companyId in the request body?

400, and nothing is created. Not “parsed and ignored” — refused at the parse, because every write schema is .strict() and companyId is not a declared field.

The distinction is the point. “Ignored” is a property of today's code: it holds only as long as nobody spreads the parsed object into a Prisma create. “Refused” is a property of the schema, and it holds whatever the service does next. The same applies to role and decision.

Can I search my way into another company's records?

No. The search term is an extra AND clause; the tenant predicate is a separate clause on the same query and always present. A search that matches another company's merchant returns nothing.

One real bug lived here and is fixed: % and _ were being passed straight into the ILIKE pattern, so ?q=% returned every expense in your own company. Never a leak — the tenant predicate still applied — but wrong results that looked right.

apps/api/src/expenses/expense-query.service.ts:185the fix
function escapeLikePattern(term: string): string {
  return term.replace(/[\\%_]/gu, (character) => `\\${character}`);
}

Backslash first, so a literal one cannot consume the character after it. The regression test does not assert “fewer rows” — a broken escape gives that too. It submits one merchant containing a literal % and another containing a literal _, and asserts each term finds its own row and not the other's, which is precisely what a wildcard cannot do.

Sending more than one request at a time

Eight identical submissions arrive at once. How many expenses exist?

Exactly one, and all eight responses name it. One request gets 201; the other seven get 200 with duplicate: true, the same expense id and the same decision.

The guarantee is the unique index, not application logic. The losers catch PostgreSQL error 23505, read the winning row and return it. There is no pre-check to lose the race.

Two managers approve the same review simultaneously.

Exactly one winner, every trial. The winner gets 200. The loser gets a 409 that names the decision that already happened, and the expense is unchanged.

One conditional UPDATE with AND state = 'OPEN' does it. PostgreSQL serialises the two updates on the row, so the second matches zero rows. No read-then-write window exists for a second manager to slip into.

Ten expenses from one employee arrive at once, against a limit of three.

This one has two honest answers, and they differ.

Identical spentAt: exactly 3 approve, 7 review — deterministically, every run. That outcome is only reachable if the ten transactions serialised, so the result itself is the evidence that the advisory lock works. A negative control removes the lock and watches all ten approve.

Skewed spentAt, milliseconds apart: three to seven approve, varying by run. This is risk R-11, and it is a real gap, not a flaky test. Arrival order and spentAt order diverge, and evaluation only looks backwards.

The test for the second case deliberately asserts no approval count — that number depends on lock order, which is undetermined, so asserting it would be flaky by construction. It asserts the two things true on every interleaving: the earliest spentAt always approves however late it arrives, and no expense's recorded count can exceed its rank in spentAt order. That bound is the limitation, written as an invariant.

Six deliveries of the same webhook event arrive together.

One event row, one expense. The delivery-layer unique index on (companyId, providerEventId) admits one; the rest are recognised as redeliveries and answered without doing the work again.

And the same transaction id under two different event ids also becomes one expense — caught by the second layer, the expense-level idempotency key. That is why one layer would not have been enough.

Attacking the webhook

I captured a valid delivery. Can I replay it?

Two independent answers. The timestamp window bounds the replay in both directions — including a request signed far in the future, which a past-only bound would leave replayable until that date arrives. And even inside the window, the delivery-layer unique index recognises the event id and creates nothing.

I changed the amount but kept the signature.

401, and nothing is stored. The HMAC covers the raw bytes, so any change to the body changes the expected signature.

The subtler version of this attack is re-serialising the same object with different whitespace. That is also refused — which is the test that proves verification is over the raw bytes rather than over a parsed object.

I signed it correctly, but with another company's secret.

401. The delivery id selects which secret to try; it proves nothing on its own. Only the holder of that secret can produce a signature that verifies, and the company is then taken from the integration whose secret verified — never from the payload, which has no company field at all.

Can I tell which check failed?

No. Twelve distinct refusal paths — wrong, malformed, empty and missing signatures; missing timestamp and delivery headers; unknown delivery id; timestamps two hours either side; a non-numeric timestamp; another company's secret; another company's delivery id — all answer 401 with an identical response body.

Timing is handled too: an unknown delivery id is compared against a dummy HMAC so the refusal takes the same work as a real one, and signatures are compared in constant time after a shape check — the shape check first, because timingSafeEqual throws on a length mismatch, which would itself be an oracle.

Can a browser be tricked into calling the webhook endpoint?

The endpoint is the one route carrying @WebhookExempt(), which turns off CSRF. That is safe for the reason CSRF exists: it protects a credential the browser attaches automatically, and this route reads none. A cross-site request with a cookie attached is a 401, because the cookie is never read and a browser cannot produce the signature.

A test asserts that exactly one route carries the decorator, so the exemption cannot quietly spread to a route that does read the session.

Malformed and hostile input

Can I make it return a 500?

Not with input, as far as has been tested. Thirty malformed submissions, eleven malformed policy updates, eight malformed company creations, five malformed manager creations, six malformed webhook payloads, four corrupt pagination cursors, four out-of-range limits, a 12 MB body, a wrong content type and truncated JSON. No 500 was produced by any input.

One case used to break this and is worth admitting: an oversized body returned 500 on every endpoint, and had done since the expense API was built. body-parser raises a rejection carrying a 413 that is not an HttpException, so the catch-all filter flattened it. It was found by a webhook size test and confirmed against POST /v1/expenses. It now answers 413 payload_too_large.

SQL injection in the search box?

The term is a bound parameter and is matched as the literal text it is. There is no string-built SQL anywhere in the codebase; the few raw statements are tagged templates where every value is a parameter.

Worth separating from the wildcard bug above: that was never injection. Binding a value stops it being SQL; it does not stop it being pattern syntax inside a LIKE.

Operations

What survives a restart?

Everything written by a user. Verified from a clean clone: companies, a manager provisioned through the API, accepted webhook events, a resolved expense still APPROVED, the company's policy unchanged, and the stored signing secret still verifying a fresh signed delivery after the stack came back.

The seed re-runs idempotently rather than doubling anything.

The worker dies halfway through an event.

The lease expires, another attempt claims the row, and the submission service finds the externalId already stored and returns the existing expense. One expense, no new code for the crash case.

This is at-least-once processing with idempotent effects. Exactly-once delivery is not claimed, because it is not achievable across a network boundary — what is achievable is that repeated processing has the same effect as processing once.

Is the login rate limited?

No. Fifteen consecutive wrong passwords all answer 401 with no throttling and no lockout, and the sixteenth correct attempt succeeds. Recorded as risk R-19, accepted and out of scope.

The partial mitigation is bcrypt at cost 10 — roughly 100 ms per attempt, so one attacker manages about ten per second per connection — and generic errors that reveal nothing about which half of the pair was wrong. A distributed attempt is unaffected.

Is there a Content-Security-Policy?

Not a real one, and the header that exists must not be mistaken for one. The web tier sends Content-Security-Policy: frame-ancestors 'none' — one directive. It governs who may frame the page, not what may execute inside it, and it does nothing for script injection. Risk R-18 is unchanged and says so explicitly.

The framing directive was added for a real gap: the session cookie is SameSite=Lax, which blocks a cross-site POST but not a framed top-level GET, so the dashboard could be loaded in an invisible iframe with a decoy over a one-click Approve. That is risk R-39, now mitigated.

The four negative controls

A passing test proves the code does something. It does not prove the mechanism is why. For the four guarantees the assessment actually grades, there is a test that removes the mechanism and watches the failure reappear.

What this system does not claim

  • No automated frontend interaction tests (R-23). Browser behaviour is verified by hand and recorded as such, never reported as a passing test.
  • No load testing. The scaling answer is architectural, and is labelled as reasoning rather than measurement.
  • No Content-Security-Policy for script injection (R-18). The header that exists carries frame-ancestors only.
  • No rate limiting on login or per API key (R-19).
  • No reconciliation against the card provider (R-38) — explained in Part 04 rather than built.
  • No audit log of administrative actions. createdAt, rotatedAt and secretVersion make the fact recoverable; who did it is not recorded.
  • No zero-risk claim. Thirteen limitations are open in the risk register, each with what it costs and what would close it.
